01 / POSITIONING

How does GPS find a location?

Real GPS satellites transmit precisely timed radio signals. A receiver compares the signal timing to estimate its distance to each satellite. Because its own clock is imperfect, the receiver solves for clock offset as well as position. Normally at least four suitable satellite measurements are needed to determine three spatial coordinates and the clock offset.

Our 2D trilateration model

On a flat map, one exact distance from a known point defines a circle. Two circles can intersect at two points; a third suitable measurement can distinguish the receiver position.

d = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]

For example, the distance from (2, 3) to (8, 11) is √(6² + 8²) = 10 units. Our gps-engine.js uses this formula and solves circle equations for the simulated receiver.

Important: The points labelled satellites in the 2D classroom map are fictional reference points, not physical GPS satellite locations or orbits. The 3D scene is a conceptual illustration, not a 3D GPS position solver.

02 / TIMING

Why do tiny delays matter?

Radio signals travel at approximately the speed of light in a vacuum: 299,792,458 metres per second. Distance is estimated from travel time, so an added delay can increase the measured range.

extra measured distance = speed of light × extra delay

100 nanoseconds = 100 × 10⁻⁹ seconds. Therefore, the added measured distance is approximately 29.98 metres.

Range error is not the same as position error. The final location estimate depends on the satellite geometry and how the different measurements are combined. Buildings can reflect signals (multipath), and the atmosphere and clocks can introduce other errors. Our slider deliberately changes one simulated range measurement only.

03 / NAVIGATION

How do computers find a route?

We represent the city as a graph: junctions are nodes and roads are connections called edges. Breadth-first search (BFS) starts at the origin and explores connected junctions in layers.

Because all road segments have equal length in our fictional city, BFS finds a route using the fewest segments. When a road closes, its connection is removed from the available choices, and the algorithm finds another route if one exists.

For East Market, the route uses six segments with the central roads open and eight when they close. Real navigation systems usually use weighted routes to account for distance, travel time and traffic.

What our model does not claim

We do not simulate real GPS orbital mechanics, radio propagation, a full 3D positioning solution, live traffic or real road distances. Our interactive 3D scenes visualise the concepts, while the labelled classroom models demonstrate the underlying mathematics.
